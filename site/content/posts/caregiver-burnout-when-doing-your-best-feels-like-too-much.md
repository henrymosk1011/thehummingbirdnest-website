Being a Caregiver can be very rewarding but it can also be quite exhausting.

Appointments to keep, routines to follow, big feelings to manage, meals to prepare, and a million little things to remember all day long. Sometimes it feels like everyone wants something from you and there is little time for yourself.

If you have ever been overwhelmed, emotionally drained, or running on empty, you are not failing. You may be facing caregiver burnout.

## What Is Caregiver Burnout?

Caregiver burnout is the physical, mental, and emotional exhaustion that can happen when the demands of caregiving exceed the time, energy, and support you have available.

Individual experiences may vary. You might notice:

* Feeling tired after getting some rest
* Getting frustrated or overwhelmed easily
* Feeling guilty when taking time for yourself
* Having less patience than usual
* Not interested in things you normally like
* Feeling like you should be doing more than you are doing
* Have trouble asking for help because you think you should be able to do it all

Sometimes burnout does not look like completely falling apart. Sometimes it feels like just making it through the day and wondering when you will get a chance to breathe.

## You Don't Have to Do Everything

Caregivers are often held to incredibly high expectations, sometimes by others but often by themselves.

You may wonder:

*Am I doing enough?*

*Should I be handling this differently?*

*Did I respond the right way?*

*Could I be doing more for my child?*

The truth is, **you do not have to do everything perfectly to be an effective caregiver**.

Your child does not need a perfect parent or caregiver. They need someone who is present, caring, and willing to keep learning alongside them.

Some days, you may have the energy to practice strategies, prepare activities, and stay calm through every challenge. Other days, getting everyone fed, dressed, and through the day may be enough.

Both days count.

## Self Compassion Is Part of Caregiving

We often talk about giving children compassion when they are struggling. We remind them that mistakes are okay, that big feelings are okay, that they can try again.

Caregivers deserve that same compassion.

Instead of thinking "I should be doing more," try "I am doing what I can with the energy and resources I have today." Instead of "I lost my patience," try "That was a difficult moment. I can reflect on it and try again."

Self compassion doesn't mean ignoring areas where you want to grow. It means allowing yourself to be human while you grow.
